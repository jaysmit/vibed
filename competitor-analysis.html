What They All Have in Common

  • Survivorship bias — You hear from founders AFTER they've succeeded (or clearly failed). The messy middle is invisible.
  • Text-heavy — Written posts that can be edited, polished, and sanitized.
  • Revenue obsession — "$10K MRR!" posts dominate. No revenue = no engagement.
  • Retrospective framing — "Here's what I learned" instead of "Here's what I'm struggling with right now."
  • No accountability — Founders share goals but there's no mechanism to track if they followed through.

Your Genuine Differentiators

  1. The Promises Feature — This is genuinely unique. Public accountability with dates creates stakes. No competitor has this.
  2. Video-First — Harder to fake, more personal, builds parasocial connection. Text posts can be ghostwritten; videos can't.
  3. Structured Comparability — Because every founder answers the same 16 questions, you can actually compare journeys. "How did founder A handle their first sale vs founder B?"
  4. The Messy Middle — You're capturing what others miss: the uncertainty, the pivots, the "I don't know if this will work" moments.

The Brutal Truth

Problems You Need to Acknowledge

1. The Chicken-and-Egg Problem
Founders won't document their journey if nobody's watching. Audiences won't come if there are no interesting founders. Every platform faces this. Most die here.

2. Video is High Friction
Writing a text post takes 10 minutes. Recording, editing, and publishing a video takes hours. You're asking founders to do MORE work than competitors, not less.

3. The "Building in Public" Fatigue
This trend peaked in 2021-2022. Many founders got burned by oversharing. The market is more skeptical now.

4. Monetization is Unclear
Product Hunt has launches. Indie Hackers has a community. What does Vibed sell? Attention alone isn't a business model.

5. Quality Control
Most startup journeys are boring. "Week 47: Still coding features nobody will use." How do you ensure content is actually compelling?

6. Competition for Founder Attention
Founders already post on Twitter/X, LinkedIn, their own blogs, YouTube, Indie Hackers, Reddit... Why add another platform?

The Real Question: Why Should You Keep Going?

Ask Yourself These Questions:

1. Are you building this because you want to USE it?
Would you, as a founder, genuinely document your journey on Vibed? If not, why would others?

2. Have you validated with real users?
Not "would you use this?" (everyone says yes). But "here's the app, document your journey for 4 weeks." Did they?

3. What's your unfair advantage?
Do you have access to founders who would use this? An existing audience? Deep expertise in video/community?

4. Can you survive the cold start?
The first 6-12 months will have almost no organic traction. Can you sustain development with no users?

What Would Make Vibed Win

If you continue, these are the things that would actually matter:

  1. Seed with 10-20 compelling founders — Not just anyone. Find founders building interesting things who are naturally good on camera. Pay them if you have to. Quality > quantity at the start.
  2. Make video creation stupidly easy — One-tap recording, auto-captions, auto-editing. The friction must be near zero.
  3. Double down on promises — This is your moat. Make the accountability feature so good that founders come just for that. Public promise tracking that integrates with their other tools.
  4. Create FOMO for followers — "You watched Founder X's first video when they had 3 followers. Now they have 50,000." Early followers should feel special.
  5. Monetize through the founders, not the audience — Founders pay for premium features, analytics, export tools. Don't rely on advertising or audience payments.

Final Verdict

6/10

The concept is solid. The execution will be brutal.

Vibed has genuine differentiation — especially the promises feature and video-first approach. These aren't gimmicks; they solve real problems with existing platforms.

But differentiation isn't enough. You're entering a crowded space where even well-funded competitors (remember Clubhouse?) can fail. The founder content market is noisy, attention-span is short, and "building in public" has become performative.

Should you keep going?

Yes, IF:

  • You can get 10+ quality founders to actively use it for 3+ months
  • You're willing to do things that don't scale (manually recruiting, personally editing videos)
  • You have 12+ months of runway without needing revenue
  • You're building this because you genuinely care about the problem, not just the market

No, IF:

  • You're hoping for organic viral growth
  • You can't get quality founders to commit before launch
  • You're building features without talking to users weekly
  • You're motivated by "this could be the next Product Hunt" rather than "I want to watch founders build"

The best products come from founders who would build them even if no one else cared. Would you?
